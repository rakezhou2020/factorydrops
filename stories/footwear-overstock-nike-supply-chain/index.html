WHY BIG FOOTWEAR SUPPLY CHAINS SOMETIMES END UP WITH TOO MUCH PRODUCT

Nike is a useful public example of how enormous modern footwear networks work — and why inventory can become a problem even when the factories, products and brands are world class.

FACTORY DROPS EDITORIAL · AUGUST 2026 · INDUSTRY REPORT
Footwear production involves many separate stages, suppliers and decisions long before a finished pair reaches a shopper. Photo: EvoMao / Unsplash.

When shoppers see a finished sneaker on a shelf, the supply chain behind it is almost invisible. A large footwear company may coordinate product design, material suppliers, contract manufacturers, logistics providers, distribution centers, wholesale partners and its own retail channels — across several countries at once.

Nike gives us a useful sense of scale. In its fiscal 2026 annual filing, the company said that nearly all of its products are manufactured by independent contractors, while its materials network also includes hundreds of strategic Tier 2 suppliers. That does not make Nike unusual. Contract manufacturing is a core part of the global footwear industry.

THE HARD PART ISN'T ONLY MAKING THE SHOE. IT'S MATCHING PRODUCTION, TIMING AND DEMAND.

One production decision can travel through a very long chain

A factory needs materials, labor, production capacity and time. A brand or buyer needs the right number of units, in the right sizes and colors, at the right moment. Retailers then need those products to arrive while consumers still want them.

When everything lines up, the system looks efficient. When one assumption changes — demand, delivery time, a promotion, a season, an order quantity or channel strategy — inventory can become harder to place.

Shoe manufacturing combines skilled labor, tooling, materials and specialized production steps. Photo: EvoMao / Unsplash.

Excess inventory is real — even at the biggest brands

Nike itself has publicly discussed excess and older inventory. In 2022, the company was dealing with unusually high inventory after transit times changed and products arrived differently than expected. More recently, Nike's turnaround has again included efforts to clean up older or unhealthy inventory in parts of its marketplace.

That matters because it shows something simple: having too much inventory does not automatically mean a product is bad. Sometimes the problem is timing, assortment, demand forecasting or distribution.

Footwear inventory becomes complicated quickly because one design can have many sizes, colors and market destinations. Photo: Sarguninder Singh / Unsplash.

But “brand excess inventory” and “factory overruns” are not the same claim

This distinction is important.

Public reporting can show that a brand uses contract factories. Public reporting can also show that the brand has, at different times, dealt with excess inventory. Those two facts do not prove that a contract factory is free to sell extra branded units on its own.

A factory may manufacture for a famous brand and still be restricted by its contracts, intellectual-property rules, quality controls or disposal requirements. The right to manufacture a product is not automatically the right to sell that product independently.

Where Factory Drops fits into this story

Our interest is not in pretending every leftover product has the same story. It doesn't.

Some inventory is ordinary overstock. Some is extra production. Some is a broken size run. Some comes from a cancelled order. Some may be unbranded. Some may involve third-party brands or licensed intellectual property and therefore require additional evidence before it should be promoted.

That is why Factory Drops is being built around a simple idea: show what we know, identify who is actually selling the product, and avoid turning incomplete information into a guarantee.

Materials, stitching, finishing and construction are part of the product story — but editorial presentation is not a substitute for seller responsibility or product inspection. Photo: Tareq Hasan / Unsplash.

Why this matters for factories

A factory can be very good at producing goods and still be bad at finding a consumer for a small leftover batch. Its systems are usually built around manufacturing orders, not storytelling, consumer acquisition, individual fulfillment or digital merchandising.

That gap is where a discovery platform can have value. Not by claiming that every piece of excess inventory is special, and not by hiding who actually sells it — but by making legitimate clearance opportunities easier to understand and easier to discover.

A CLEARANCE PROBLEM IS OFTEN A MATCHING PROBLEM: THE PRODUCT EXISTS, BUT THE RIGHT BUYER IS SOMEWHERE ELSE.

Our takeaway

Large footwear supply chains are efficient because they operate at scale. That same scale also makes forecasting mistakes, timing shifts and inventory imbalances expensive.

Nike's public filings and inventory history illustrate the size and complexity of that system. They do not establish that unauthorized branded factory leftovers are legitimate for resale. For Factory Drops, that distinction will remain important as we decide what information to publish and what inventory belongs on the platform.
